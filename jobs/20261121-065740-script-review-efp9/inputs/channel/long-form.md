# Long-form lectures: Postal Exams

One lecture, one topic unit, 8 to 20 minutes. Lectures belong to a series (Postal Knowledge, Savings and Insurance, Service Rules, Maths, Reasoning, GK, Exam Guide). Hinglish is spoken at about 130 to 140 words a minute, so a 12-minute lecture is about 1,600 words of script. Every fact comes from the owner's material when the lecture is made from it (R9).

## Shape

| Part | Timing (12-min lecture) | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Topic and big picture | 0:00–0:40 | The topic named plainly, one line of background where it helps (when it began), and the whole idea in one or two sentences. |
| Map | 0:55–1:15 | The parts, numbered. The section labels follow these names. |
| Part 1 | 1:15–4:00 | Idea → meaning → an example where it's needed → exam angle. |
| Part 2 | 4:00–6:45 | Same pattern. State the current rule only; no old-vs-new comparison. |
| Recap card | ~6:45 | Three to five keywords from Parts 1 and 2. |
| Part 3 | 6:45–9:30 | Same pattern. |
| याद रखने वाली बातें | 9:30–10:45 | A short, plain recap of the five or so points that matter most (the material's key points first), in simple sentences, the way a teacher sums up; each confusion said as the correct fact (R3), not as a separate fact list or trap list. |
| Close | 11:45–12:00 | One warm line. No mention of other lectures (R14). |

For 8 minutes, use two parts; for 20 minutes, four or five. Keep a recap at least every 7 minutes.

**Lectures teach only (owner, Oct 2026):** no quick checks, no MCQs, no practice set, no "video रोकिए, comment में लिखिए". Practice questions in the source material (MCQs, flashcards) are for the owner's study app, not for the video.

## Rules

### R1 · Open on the topic, plainly
**When:** The first 40 seconds.
**Do:** Name the topic the way the syllabus names it. Add one line of background where it helps (when it began, what it is). Give the whole picture in one or two sentences. Then the parts. No hook, puzzle, surprise question or story at the start; no greeting or channel intro.
**Why:** The owner's decision after a viewer's feedback (Oct 2026): a lecture that opened on a Daman puzzle left a newcomer lost. A student first wants to know what they are studying and where it starts. No line about which exams the lecture is for (owner, Nov 2026): the videos are watched inside the owner's study app, which is built for these exams.
**Example:** "आज का topic है Department of Posts का organisation। Department of Posts की शुरुआत 1 October 1854 को हुई। आज ये Ministry of Communications के नीचे है, और पूरे देश में 23 Circles में बँटा है। हर Circle के नीचे Regions, Divisions और Sub-divisions हैं। इस lecture में हम ऊपर से नीचे तक यही ढाँचा पढ़ेंगे।"

### R2 · Teach in the natural order, like a good teacher
**When:** The whole lecture.
**Do:**
- Go from big to small and from general to particular, the way a good textbook does: usually the source material's own order.
- Bring in each idea before its exceptions, traps and special cases; a trap comes inside the part it belongs to, after the idea.
- No story running through the lecture, no single example followed from start to end, no catchphrase line repeated across parts.

**Why:** The owner's decision (Oct 2026): this channel teaches simply and effectively. A story thread and a surprise order make a newcomer work to follow; a clear order lets them build the picture step by step.

### R3 · Idea, meaning, exam angle
**When:** Every part.
**Do:**
- State the idea in one line and explain it plainly. Where it needs one, give an example (R4). Then the exam angle: what to remember and how the MCQ options try to trap you. Then move on.
- **A trap is taught once, where its idea is.** Say the correct fact, and name the wrong version the options use once ("option में 4 घंटे minimum लिखा हो, तो गलत"). After that, say only the correct fact: in the recap and anywhere it comes back, "BO: 5 घंटे maximum", never the wrong version again.

**Why:** A student remembers an idea they understood, and the exam angle tells them what the paper will ask. A wrong number heard three or four times ("4 घंटे का minimum नहीं") sticks better than the right one (owner's review of lectures 1–3, Nov 2026).

### R4 · Examples where they help understanding
**When:** An idea is hard to picture or easy to confuse: a chain of offices, a rule with conditions, a calculation, two terms that sound alike, an exception.
**Do:**
- Give an example that makes the idea concrete, right after the idea it explains.
- First the material's own examples. They are part of the material, so every one is used (R5), in the part where it belongs, said plainly.
- Where the material has no example and the idea needs one, give a simple one from postal work or daily life. It only illustrates: it adds no new fact, number, name or office presented as real (R9).
- One good example is enough. An idea that is already clear needs none; don't pad.

**Why:** The owner's rule (Nov 2026): an example is often what makes a rule click for a newcomer, and the material's own examples were chosen for that.
**Example:** "BO का account SO में जाता है। जैसे गाँव की दुकान का हिसाब शहर के wholesaler के पास। Exam में trap: option में लिखा होगा 'BO, HO को report करता है' — गलत।"

### R5 · Cover everything in the material, briefly
**When:** The whole lecture, and especially when it is made from source material.
**Do:**
- The material is the base of the lecture. Every concept, fact, number, date, name, definition, distinction, example, correction and trap in it goes into the script; nothing is dropped to save time.
- Give the time by exam value. The material marks what the exam asks most: each section's list of key points (headed "Prelims focus" or "प्रारंभिक परीक्षा के मुख्य बिंदु" in the owner's packages). Build each part on those points: they get the clearest explanation, an example where it helps, and a place in the closing recap. Background and minor details get one line each, but they are said.
- **Departmental postal exams have no prelims or mains** (owner, Nov 2026): that heading only means "key exam points". Never say prelims, mains, प्रारंभिक or मुख्य परीक्षा, in the voice or on screen.
- Get to each point quickly: no long build-up and no restating a point that is already clear (what not to explain: R6). Repeat only the facts that are easy to confuse. The length follows from that.

**Why:** The owner's feedback (Oct 2026): the first full script was accurate and complete but too long, because it explained basics the viewers already know; and the material is what the lecture is made from, so all of it must be there. The review of lectures 1–3 (Nov 2026): every fact got the same weight, so a student couldn't tell what matters most.

### R6 · Explain only what's new, never the basics
**When:** A term, abbreviation or rule name appears.
**Do:** Everyday postal terms the viewers already know (post office, BO/SO/HO, Circle, Division, Speed Post, GDS, MTS, CPMG) are used as they are, without explaining them. Explain a term only when most candidates won't know it, when it's technical, or when the exam builds a trap on it: say it as the paper writes it, one plain line, and a key-term card.
**Why:** The viewers already know most of the basics. Explaining what they know makes the lecture long and slow; their time should go to the exam facts, distinctions and traps.
**Example:** "Mis-sent article — मतलब वो article जो sorting में गलत office भेज दिया गया।"

### R7 · Write for the ear, in Hinglish
**When:** Every line.
**Do:** Most sentences under 12 words, one idea each. Write Hindi in Devanagari and English terms in English letters. Use everyday Hindi, not shuddh. End each sentence with "।" or "?". Make it easy for an AI voice: no clause numbers like rule 2(1)(c) or section 3(2) in spoken lines (say the idea; the exact number goes on a screen card in Production notes), no slashes or "&" (say "और", "या", "and").
**Numbers are said at most once** (owner, Nov 2026): a rule, regulation, clause or section number may be spoken once, where the rule is first explained, only if it helps; never stacked in one sentence or repeated. Old Guide clauses and old rule numbers are not mentioned: state only the current rule.
A definition from an Act or Rule is said in everyday words first (what it means at the office), then its term; never as a translated legal sentence ("perpetual succession और common seal वाली body corporate"). The exact words go on the card (R8).
**Why:** Viewers listen after a long day. Short, familiar sentences are followed without rewinding. English terms in English letters keep the voice timing exact. Translated legal Hindi was the hardest part of lectures 1–3 to follow by ear.
**Except:** Exact rule text on a card stays in its official words; the voice explains it in short sentences.

### R8 · Exact words on a card, plain words in the voice
**When:** Any rule, section, definition or formula.
**Do:**
- Show the exact text on a document card with its source (act or rule, number, year).
- Show the official Hindi beside it if it exists, or else a short Hindi gist labelled "सार".
- Highlight the phrase being explained.
- Never read the card aloud word for word.

**Why:** MCQs turn on single words ("shall", "may", "30 days"). Reading a card aloud wastes the viewer's time.

### R9 · Every fact from the material, taught as it is now
**When:** Every number, limit, rate, rule number, section, date and exam detail.
**Do:**
- When the lecture is made from the owner's material, the material is the only source. Every fact, number, date, name, example given as fact and exam claim comes from it. Nothing is added from outside: not from memory, other books or the internet. No margin notes are needed: the source is always the material.
- A lecture without material notes each fact's source in the margin; if no source can be found, the fact is cut.
- Teach the current position as plain fact: no "as of October 2026", "इस समय", "अभी तक" or "… की स्थिति में" qualifiers, in the voice or on screen. When a fact changes, the owner updates the script (owner, Nov 2026). Dates of events stay: when something began, changed or was replaced.
- **Two figures for the same thing** (an older count and the current one): say only the current one, as plain fact. The older figure is not mentioned.
- **A line in the material that would puzzle a student** (a trap given with no reason, a list that doesn't match its count, a rename that's missing from the list it explains): add one plain line that makes it make sense, using only the material. If the material can't explain it, teach it as given and list it in Production notes under "For the owner to check", so the owner can look at the source.
- Never say "ये exam में आया था" unless the material (or, without material, a cited past paper) says so.

**Why:** Wrong answers are this audience's biggest complaint about other channels. Accuracy is our reason to exist. In lecture 2, two network totals without "which one" left students unsure; in lecture 1, a list of 7 Board Members missed the post whose rename explained the 7.

### R10 · Current rule only
**When:** Any rule, limit or product that changed (1898 Act → 2023 Act; 1933 Rules → 2024 Rules and Regulations; Registered Post → Speed Post add-on; local-language paper removed from PA/SA in 2024).
**Do:** State the current rule as plain fact. No old-vs-new cards, no struck-through old versions, no sentence that compares an old rule with a new one. Where a rule was replaced, keep only the new one. A rule that did not change is stated as the current rule, without "earlier" framing. Dates of events stay once, where the change began.
**Why:** Owner's brief, Nov 2026: fewer numbers and comparisons, and the current rule is what the exam asks.

### R11 · Three new facts a minute, key fact twice
**When:** The whole script, checked per minute of voice.
**Do:**
- No more than three new facts in any minute. Say the key fact of each part twice, in different words.
- **A list of more than five items** (Board Members, Circles, holidays, documents): the whole list goes on a screen table (describe it in Production notes). The voice says how many there are, names the groups, and names only the one or two items the exam turns on. Never read the whole list aloud.

**Why:** On a bilingual screen, each fact also takes reading time. A list of 7 to 23 items read aloud is not remembered; a table the student can pause on is.

### R12 · No quizzes inside a lecture
**When:** Every lecture.
**Do:** Teach only. No quick-check questions, no MCQs, no practice set, no asking viewers to pause and comment. Where a trap matters, state it as a fact ("Exam में यहीं confusion होता है: …").
**Why:** The owner's decision (Oct 2026): lectures are for teaching. The source's MCQs are for the owner's app.

### R13 · Neutral on service disputes
**When:** Pay, TRCA, GDS status, union demands, transfers, or court cases about staff.
**Do:** State the rule and the official position, with its source. If there is a live dispute, say it exists and give each side's stated position, with sources. Never take sides.
**Why:** Viewers come from every cadre and union. The channel teaches the exam, not a position.

### R14 · One series: build on it, never contradict it
**When:** Any topic an earlier lecture already taught, and any mention of other lectures.
**Do:**
- `inputs/earlier/` holds the channel's earlier lectures, in order (when there are any). Read their headings, and read any part that touches this topic.
- A topic an earlier lecture already taught in full gets a short recap here (a few lines with its key facts), not a second full lesson. It still counts as covered (R5).
- **Never mention another lecture in the narration** (owner, Nov 2026): no "Business hours वाले lecture में", "पिछले lecture में", "हमने पहले देखा था" or "अगले lecture में". The recap is taught as plain facts, as if for the first time, just shorter.
- Never contradict an earlier lecture. Where two true facts sound opposite (a BO's opening hours have no minimum, but a GDS works at least 4 hours a day), say in one line how they fit together.

**Why:** Lectures 2 and 3 taught business hours and Night Post Offices twice, and lecture 3's "no 4-hour minimum" sounded like a contradiction of lecture 2's GDS hours (review, Nov 2026). A student watches the whole series.
